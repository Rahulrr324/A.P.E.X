INTERPRETATION AND EVIDENCE REVIEW (post-decision analysis)

WHAT THE v3 DATA ACTUALLY SHOWS

P1/P2 (PDH counter capability):
  - The PDH '% Processor Performance' counter CAN collect at 4-5 us cadence
    (417,710 samples in 2 s idle probe).
  - The counter responds to a real frequency step in 0.048 ms (P2 step test).
  - THEREFORE the counter is fundamentally capable of sub-millisecond
    resolution. The '100 ms sampler catches ~4 samples' statement from the
    earlier report is TRUE FOR THE 100 MS SAMPLER, but is NOT a limitation of
    the PDH counter itself.

  - HOWEVER: at 4-5 us cadence the counter is NOISY. 389,862 of 417,710
    samples (93.3%) differ from their predecessor by a detectable amount.
    Plateau durations are 14-28 us (4-6 samples). This means the raw %perf
    reading fluctuates by >1% of the run mean on a ~5 us timescale even when
    the actual CPU frequency is stable. These are PDH representational-noise
    artifacts, not real frequency changes.

P3 B7 (the only bench with exec-only coverage):
  - B7 gap=yes: 24,096 usable samples inside exec, median 209.333%.
  - B7 gap=no : 16,919 usable samples inside exec, median 209.333%.
  - The TWO exec-only MEDIANS ARE IDENTICAL to 6 decimal places.
    This is the single most important data point in the entire diagnostic.
  - The exec-only means differ by 0.096% (211.618 vs 211.416) -- negligible.
  - The exec-only spread is 56-86% but this is ENTIRELY PDH noise artifacts:
    * artifact_rate ~9.6-9.7% of in-exec samples are outside [20,240] (R2 filter)
    * 9,648-11,674 'transitions' detected by the +/-1% plateau rule are
      sub-percent PDH fluctuations, not genuine frequency steps.
    * The per-iteration mean %perf (3 iterations with coverage each) has
      stdev 0.08-0.33% -- i.e. the underlying level is stable to <0.5%.
  - CONCLUSION for B7: within-execution frequency is STABLE at 209.33%.
    The 56-86% 'spread' is measurement noise, not frequency variation.

P3 B6/B9 (NO exec-only coverage -- the diagnostic design flaw):
  - All 8 B6/B9 execs: n_collector_samples=0, n_samples_in_exec=0,
    n_samples_usable=0.
  - Root cause (design flaw, not PDH limitation):
    * Process affinity is restricted to CPU0 (verified_single_cpu=1).
    * The collector thread is pinned to the 'last logical CPU' per addendum R1,
      but SetThreadAffinityMask cannot grant a thread a CPU outside the
      process affinity mask. With process mask = {CPU0}, the collector is
      pinned to CPU0 -- the SAME CPU as the measured thread.
    * B6/B9 are pure-compute (matrix multiply), keeping CPU0 at ~100%
      utilization for the entire 6.6 ms exec. The collector tight-loop on
      the same CPU is starved and collects nothing.
    * B7 is file-I/O (ifstream read + deserialize), where the CPU is idle
      during I/O waits. The collector gets CPU time and collects 16,919-
      26,698 samples.
  - The PDH counter is NOT the bottleneck. The bottleneck is that the
    collector cannot run on a different CPU than the measured thread under
    the CPU0-only process affinity.

INDIRECT EVIDENCE FOR B6/B9 (why we believe frequency was also stable):
  - B6 timing: median 130-142 us across 6 execs (spread 8.45%), but
    WITHIN-EXEC drift is 0-1.5% (first25 vs last25). The within-exec
    timing is stable; the between-exec timing variation is small.
  - B9 timing: 130 us both execs (spread 0%), drift 0.8%. Perfectly stable.
  - B7 (which DOES have exec-only frequency data) shows stable 209.33%
    frequency despite 2.4% timing spread. This demonstrates that the
    High-Performance scheme holds frequency stable during execs.
  - The B6/B9 timing stability (within-exec drift <2%) is consistent with
    stable frequency, because a 5% frequency swing would produce ~5% timing
    swing, which is not observed.
  - CAVEAT: this is indirect. We cannot PROVE B6/B9 frequency was stable
    without direct exec-only samples. But all available evidence points that
    way.

THE 6.8974% EXEC-WINDOW SPREAD -- WHERE DOES IT COME FROM?
  The exec-window metric is (max-min)/max of the PDH snapshot taken BEFORE
  the exec and AFTER the exec. This is NOT an exec-only measurement.
  The snapshots capture the frequency state at two instants bracketing the
  exec, and the reported 'exec-window %perf' is effectively an average of
  those two instants (roughly).

  From the v3 data and the prior power_control_gate.txt:
    exec#8 B7 gap=yes:  before=197.013%, exec-only median=209.333%
    exec#9 B7 gap=no :  before=218.139%, exec-only median=209.333%
  The BEFORE-snapshot values (197%, 218%) are FAR from the exec-internal
  level (209.33%). The frequency at the instant of the before-snapshot is
  different from the frequency during the exec.

  This means:
    (a) The CPU frequency DOES change between execs (real behavior under
        the OS power scheduler, even in High-Performance mode).
    (b) These changes happen BETWEEN execs, not WITHIN execs.
    (c) The exec-window metric (before/after snapshots) captures these
        between-exec changes, inflating the apparent 'exec' spread.
    (d) The 6.8974% exec-window spread is therefore a SAMPLING-WINDOW
        artifact: it measures frequency variation that exists at the
        snapshot instants but NOT during the exec itself.

WHY THE PREDECLARED RULE YIELDS C (NOT B)
  The predeclared rule says: if coverage <10/exec, classify C.
  8 of 10 execs have 0 coverage. So the formal result is C.
  This is correct and conservative: we cannot PROVE the B6/B9 frequency
  was stable because we have no direct measurement. The strong indirect
  evidence (B7 exec-only data, B6/B9 timing stability) points to B, but
  the rule is designed to prevent exactly this kind of 'strong suspicion
  without proof' from being classified as B.

6. WHETHER PDH SAMPLING WAS ADEQUATE
   PDH COUNTER: ADEQUATE for sub-millisecond resolution. The counter responds
   in 0.048 ms and can be sampled at 4-5 us cadence. The earlier report's
   claim that 'the 100 ms sampler catches only about four samples during
   millisecond-scale executions' is TRUE FOR THE 100 MS SAMPLER BUT NOT for
   the high-resolution collector -- the 100 ms sampler is a different, coarser
   method.

   PDH COUNTER NOISE: The counter is NOISY at high resolution. 93.3% of
   consecutive samples differ by a detectable amount; plateau durations are
   14-28 us. This noise makes raw per-sample %perf unreliable as a frequency
   indicator. The MEDIAN over thousands of samples is stable (209.333%), but
   the spread (max-min)/max of raw samples is 56-86% -- entirely noise.
   This is a PDH representational-noise limitation, not a sampling-cadence
   limitation.

   COLLECTOR DESIGN: INADEQUATE for compute-bound execs. The collector
   thread cannot run on a different CPU than the measured thread under the
   CPU0-only process affinity. For B6/B9 (pure compute, 100% CPU0 utilization),
   the collector is starved and collects zero in-exec samples. For B7 (file I/O,
   CPU idle during I/O), the collector works and collects 16,919-26,698
   samples. This is a diagnostic design limitation, not a PDH limitation.

================================================================================
RECOMMENDED NEXT ENGINEERING ACTION (diagnosis-driven, not G7)
================================================================================

The v3 diagnostic PROVED that the PDH counter has sufficient temporal
resolution (4-5 us cadence, 0.048 ms step response) and that within-execution
frequency IS stable under High Performance for the B7 benchmark (209.333%
identical medians). What remains missing is direct exec-only measurement of
the B6/B9 compute benchmarks.

To get that data, the collector starvation must be fixed. Viable approaches
 (in preference order, all within the task's 'no undocumented MSRs, no BIOS,
no unsafe kernel mechanisms' constraint):

  (a) Run the collector on a different process-wide CPU. If the process
      affinity is expanded to include at least 2 logical CPUs, the collector
      can be pinned to the non-measured CPU and will not be starved. This
      changes the diagnostic's PROCESS affinity, not the benchmark's thread
      affinity (the measured thread stays on CPU0). The production benchmark
      semantics are preserved (CPU0-only measurement thread).

  (b) Collect PDH on the main thread between iterations. Since QPC
      brackets already exist around each op(), a PDH sample can be taken
      immediately after each t1 (before the next t0). This gives one sample
      per iteration (50 samples/exec for B6/B9) at ~130 us spacing. This is
      coarser than the tight-loop collector but far finer than 100 ms, and
      avoids the starvation problem entirely (no second thread).

  (c) Use a different PDH counter that may be less noisy. '\Processor
      Information(0,0)\Processor Frequency' (nominal frequency in MHz) may
      have different noise characteristics. Worth testing but not guaranteed
      to be better.

Approach (b) is the lowest-risk, fastest path to B6/B9 exec-only data
because it requires no affinity changes and uses the existing per-iteration
QPC brackets. Approach (a) is the highest-resolution path if process affinity
 can be safely expanded for the diagnostic.

With B6/B9 exec-only data, the classification can be upgraded from C to B
 (if frequency is confirmed stable, as B7 suggests), and the 5% gate's
validity can be re-examined with a clean measurement methodology.

G7 remains NOT AUTHORIZED until the 5% gate is satisfied with a reliable
measurement. This diagnostic has shown that the current measurement is
unreliable for computing the gate metric; fixing the measurement is the
correct next step, not imposing another power-state control.
